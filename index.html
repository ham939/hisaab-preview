The gap

Between a billing app and a six-month project, there is nothing.

Pakistan has plenty of cheap retail billing software, and plenty of real ERP sold as a consulting project. A factory with 20–250 staff sits between the two, and today runs on a billing app plus Excel plus a paper register plus WhatsApp. That is the business this replaces.

The day

The day this is built around.

This is our model of how the work actually goes, from the raw material arriving at the gate to the money arriving in the account. Every screen we build has to fit somewhere on it. If your day does not look like this, that is the single most useful thing you can tell us.

  1. 01 You order material A purchase order to the supplier. Nothing has moved yet and nothing is owed yet — the order is a promise, not a fact, and the books stay untouched until something physically arrives.
  2. 02 Material arrives The storekeeper records what actually came, which is often not what was ordered. Stock goes up and a liability appears in the same instant, or neither does. Short delivery, over delivery and the wrong item are the normal case, not the exception.
  3. 03 The supplier's bill Matched against what arrived and what was ordered. Where the three disagree, the difference is shown rather than quietly absorbed.
  4. 04 Production starts A recipe says what a batch should consume. The supervisor presses Start Production, and material moves out of the store and into work-in-progress.
  5. 05 Material is issued What the recipe suggested, edited to what was really used. The gap between the two is the number nobody currently has.
  6. 06 Production finishes Good pieces, rejected pieces and scrap, entered on one screen. If entering the spoilage is awkward, the spoilage number will be wrong forever — so it is one field on the screen you are already on, not a form somebody has to go and find.
  7. 07 Finished goods land Now costed with what the batch really consumed, including what was wasted. This is the number the whole system exists to produce: what a unit actually cost you to make.
  8. 08 A customer orders On credit, usually. Their outstanding balance and their limit are on the screen before the order is taken, not discovered a month later.
  9. 09 You invoice Stock out, revenue in, cost of sale, sales tax and the FBR submission — all in one transaction. Either every one of those happens or none of them does.
  10. 10 Money comes in Part payments, against several invoices, weeks late. This is the normal case here and most software treats it as the exception.
  11. 11 You find out what you earned Not at year end, from an accountant, six months after you could have done anything about it.

The idea

Stock is never a number. It is a list of things that happened.

Most software keeps one figure per item and overwrites it. When it drifts — and it always drifts — nobody can say when or why. Here, every movement is written down and kept, and the balance is worked out from the list. A mistake is never rubbed out; it is reversed, and both lines stay. Press the buttons.

Why it matters to you. The average cost above is what a unit of that material is worth in your store right now, blending the cheap lot and the dear lot in the proportion you actually hold. When it is issued to production, it is issued at that cost. That is how the cost of a finished piece stops being a guess.

Watch what happens when you reverse. The line does not disappear. A second, opposite line appears beside it and both stay in the record forever. Nobody can quietly make a bad month look better, and an auditor can always see what was changed and when.

And it is checkable. Because the balance is only ever a sum of the list, it can be recomputed from scratch at any time and compared with what is stored. If those two ever disagree, something is wrong and we can prove it — rather than finding out because a customer's stock figure looks odd.

The arithmetic in this little panel is the same rule the real engine uses. The real one works in exact fixed-point decimals, never in the rounding arithmetic a browser does, because money that is out by a paisa a thousand times is out by ten rupees.

The rules

Things we have decided not to do.

Each of these costs us work. They are here because the alternative costs you money or trust.

Nothing financial ever silently disappears

A correction writes a reversing entry. Deleting a posted invoice is not a feature we will add, however often it is asked for, because the day you need to prove what happened is the day it matters.

It keeps selling when the internet drops

The counter keeps working through an outage and catches up afterwards. Pakistan had week-long nationwide slowdowns and evening loadshedding this year, and the tax rules grant no exemption for either.

FBR invoicing is included, not an add-on

A software vendor does not need a licence to do this — the business integrates through a licensed integrator, and the government's own integrator does it free. Others charge for it. We will not.

AI never computes a number in your accounts

It can find you a report and fill in the filters. The database produces the figure, and you can open the query it came from. A number no one can trace is worse than no number.

No implementation project

Free ERP already exists. What you cannot get is one that works on Monday without a partner, a server and three months. If we ever need a project to get you started, we have lost the only real argument we have.

One business cannot see another

Three separate locks, and a test suite that tries to break through every one of them on every table, every time we change anything. This is the mistake that would end the company, so it is tested like it.

Over to you

Where we are probably wrong.

Everything above was built from research, not from a factory. These are the specific things we have guessed at. We are not asking which features you want — we are asking how the work actually goes, because the features follow from that and never the other way round.

Walk us through yesterday, from the gate to the bank.

Not how it should work. What actually happened, including the part that went wrong and what somebody did about it.

What do you write on paper that no software has ever replaced?

The register, the gate slip, the diary in the drawer. Whatever survived every previous attempt is the thing that matters most.

When material is short-delivered, who finds out, and when?

We have assumed the storekeeper records it as it lands. If it is really reconciled a week later against a delivery challan, our whole receiving screen is wrong.

How do you know today what a finished piece cost you?

If the honest answer is "roughly" or "at year end", say so. That is the number we think is worth paying for, and we would like to know if we are right.

What percentage is wasted, and how do you know?

And who would be embarrassed by that number appearing on a screen? That question decides whether the feature works or is quietly avoided.

Who owes you the most right now, and how long did it take you to answer that?

If the answer came from a phone call to your accountant, that is the thing to fix first.

What happens on a day the internet is down?

Do you stop billing, bill on paper, or carry on and fix it later? We have built for the third and we may be building for a habit you do not have.

Who at your factory will refuse to use this?

There is always one, and they are usually right about something. Naming them now is cheaper than discovering them at go-live.

What did you try before, and why did it stop being used?

The month it was abandoned, and what people went back to. That story predicts our failure better than any feature list.
